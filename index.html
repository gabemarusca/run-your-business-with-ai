AI workflow workbook.

Take-home workbook

Build one workflow.

You need one home base, and it is the AI app you already pay for. Every step of the method runs inside that one window.

Whenever you do not know how to set something up, your first prompt is:

Master habit explain how this works, then interview me.
01

Core path

The six steps

Work in order. Build one workflow and keep your approval on anything another person will read.

Step 1

Track where your time actually goes.

Reconstruct yesterday when a full tracking week is not available.

Time audit, reconstructed"I want to find out where my working time actually goes, and I'd like you to reconstruct yesterday with me. Interview me one question at a time, starting with when I sat down to work, using my calendar, my sent emails, and my memory as sources. Build a list of everything I did and roughly how long each thing took, and mark whether it happens once or repeats through the week. When we're done, show me the repeating tasks ranked by how much time they likely eat per week, and flag any task I described with frustration in my wording, because those are probably the ones I shouldn't be doing myself."

The honest version, on paper

If the reconstruction feels thin, or your weeks look nothing alike, track it by hand. A table with four columns: activity name, start time, estimated time, actual stop time. Keep it for at least a week, ideally a month, because one week lies; a month shows the pattern. Then paste the table into the time-audit prompt above and let your AI find what repeats.

Step 2

Sort what repeats.

Delete, delegate, automate fully, or let AI draft and you finish.

Brain dump to sorted piles"Here is a brain dump of everything I do in a normal week in my business: [paste notes or voice-note transcript]. Break it into individual tasks. For each one, tell me whether it repeats, roughly what it costs me per week in time, and which bucket it belongs in: delete (nobody actually needs this anymore), delegate to a person (needs human judgment or relationships), automate fully (a wrong result would cost minutes), or automate as a draft I review (another human reads it with my name on it). Where you can't decide, ask me clarifying questions one at a time. Finish with the one task you'd automate first: the fully automatable one that eats the most time."
Pick the one"Here is the list of repeating tasks we just built. Rank the top five by highest impact against lowest setup effort, and for each one tell me: the hours a week it gives back, how hard it is to describe step by step (easy, medium, hard), and what actually improves in my business if it goes away, in plain words, not jargon. Then pick one and tell me why it beats the other four. If your pick isn't the biggest time sink, say why you chose it anyway."
Human handoff"I need to hand [task] to a person instead of doing it myself. Interview me one question at a time until you know everything they'd need: what comes in, the steps, what good looks like, the rules, common mistakes, and what they should escalate to me instead of deciding alone. Then write it up as a one-page working guide for a capable new hire, in plain language, with a checklist at the end they can follow on their first day."
Step 3

Give the machine eyes.

Connect only what the task needs.

From the workshop"The AI doesn't know my business."

Of course it doesn't. Nothing was connected, so it knew nothing because it was given nothing. The moment you connect one source, let's say your inbox, it stops guessing and starts reading. Small access, small mistakes: one connector is enough for your first workflow.

Setup navigator"I use [ChatGPT / Claude] on a paid plan, and I want to automate this task: [one sentence]. Tell me exactly where in your product I should build this: a normal chat, a Project, the desktop agent, or a scheduled task, and then walk me through the setup one step at a time, waiting for me to confirm each step before giving me the next. Include which connectors or folder access I need to turn on for this task, and just as important, which ones I should leave off. Assume I have never done any of this before."
Step 4

Build one workflow.

The briefing is the SOP, and the AI interviews you to write it.

From the workshop"It gives me generic output."

Generic output comes from a clever prompt instead of a briefing. Nobody briefs a new assistant in one sentence and fires them the same afternoon, yet that's how most people treat these tools. The interview below is the fix: the machine writes its own instructions, you correct them, and the corrections stay.

Self-briefing interview"I want you to handle [task] for me on a regular basis, and I want you to write your own instructions for it. Interview me one question at a time, never more than one, until you know: what comes in and from where, the exact steps I currently follow, what a finished result I'd be happy with looks like, the rules and edge cases I already know about, and what you should do when you're unsure, where the default answer is stop and ask me. Then write the complete instructions as if you were briefing an assistant on their first day, show them to me, and revise them based on my corrections until I say they're final. Format the final version as a block I can paste into this Project's instructions."
Brain dump to briefing"Here is everything I know about how I do [task], in no particular order: [paste the dump, an existing SOP, or a voice-note transcript]. Turn this into a complete set of instructions written as if briefing an assistant on their first day: what comes in, the steps in order, what a good finished result looks like, the rules, and what to do when unsure (default: stop and ask me). Then list what's still missing or ambiguous as questions for me, and after I answer, produce the final version as a block I can paste into this Project's instructions."
Teach by example"Here are real examples of this task done to my standard: [paste 2 or 3]. Study them and describe the pattern you see: structure, tone, length, what's always included, what's never included. Then add that pattern to your instructions so every future result matches it, and use this exact format for every response."
Step 5

Run it alongside yourself for a week.

Every gap is a missing instruction.

Parallel-week gap closer"Here is what you produced today for [task], and here is what I produced doing the same task by hand: [paste both]. List every meaningful difference between them, tell me for each one which instruction was missing or unclear that caused it, and rewrite your instructions with those gaps fixed. Keep everything that already works unchanged."
Step 6

Schedule it and review a sample weekly.

Your name means your approval.

Scheduled task skeleton"Every weekday at [time], read every email that arrived since the previous run. Sort them into: needs a decision from me, needs a reply (draft one for my review, never send anything), useful information (one line each), and everything else (one line on why it can wait). If anything looks urgent or unusual, put it at the top and tell me why. Deliver the whole thing as one message, in exactly this format: [paste one example of the summary the way you want it]."
Scheduled task, generic frame"Every [cadence] at [time], [gather these inputs from these named places]. [Process them with these steps]. Deliver the result to [one fixed place], in exactly this format: [example]. Never [send / publish / delete] anything; anything that needs an action from me goes in a section called Needs my decision."
Weekly quality review"Here are [3 to 5] outputs you produced this week for [task]: [paste or attach]. Compare them against your instructions and against this example of the standard: [paste]. Tell me: where you followed the instructions, where you drifted, and what in the instructions should be tightened to stop the drift. Do not defend the outputs; I want the honest gap list."
Revive a workflow"You run [task] for me and the quality has dropped, or the scheduled run stopped. Here are the last good output and the most recent one: [paste both]. First, diagnose what changed: the inputs, the instructions, or the format drift. Second, check whether your instructions still cover the current shape of the inputs, and rewrite the outdated parts. Third, give me the final corrected instructions as one block I can re-save, because re-saving is also how a silently stopped scheduled task gets revived."
02

SOP extraction

Start where work breaks.

People can describe a failure in detail. Use that detail to find the process stored in someone’s head.

Three failure questions

  1. Where do things most often go wrong or arrive late: a handoff between two people, two tools that don't talk, or a client waiting?
  2. What only one person in your business knows how to do?
  3. What question do you answer over and over, in slightly different words, for different people?

Mark every step

LightRuleExamples
GreenHand it over completely. Wrong output costs minutes.Sorting, summarizing, transcribing, research, formatting.
YellowMachine drafts, you finish and send. Wrong output costs trust.Emails, proposals, posts, client reports.
RedKeep the decision with you.Pricing, saying no, apologies, negotiation.
From the workshop"My work is too custom to automate."

True for the whole job, false for every task inside it. That's exactly why you mark each step: needs me, needs a human, needs nobody. The judgment stays with you; the sorting, formatting, summarizing and so on never needed you in the first place.

03

Where work happens

Answer. Work. Machine.

In the chat window you get an answer. In the workspace you get work. In the code surface you get a machine. Every failed AI story comes from asking the answer window to do the work of the other two.

SurfaceOn ClaudeOn ChatGPTWhat you get
Chat windowClaude chatChatGPT chatAn answer. Questions, single drafts, thinking out loud. This is the sounding board, and it's only one room in the building.
WorkspaceCowork and DesignWork modeWork. It holds your instructions and files, runs multi-step jobs, and produces finished pieces you review.
Code surfaceClaude CodeCodexA machine. It remembers, improves, and touches your own files. One evening to set up, or delegate the setup.

One honest limitation to know: neither platform reacts to events in real time. Nothing fires the moment an email arrives. Scheduled runs are the workaround: they run on a clock, at most once per hour, checking and notifying you only when something meaningful changed. That's enough for everything in this workbook.

The model rule

The surface decides where the work happens. The model decides how much thinking it gets.

Match the brain to the cost of a mistake, not to the size of the task: the fast, cheap model sorts and summarizes, and the big one writes anything a client reads with your name on it.

One home base

  1. Pick one home base: the AI app you already pay for.
  2. Bring your tools into it. You never go out to your tools; your tools come in to it.
  3. Add a specialist only when a task demands it.

And so you know: my own setup runs several models through a custom harness, and some days it overwhelms me too. That's my lab, not my recommendation. If you can write a note to a new assistant and read their draft the next morning, you already have every technical skill this method needs.

From the workshop"It's just a sounding board."

That's the chat window talking, and I use it as a sounding board too, don't imagine I don't. But the chat window is one room in the building. The workspace gives you work, the code surface gives you a machine, and most people judge the whole building without ever leaving the first room.

From the workshop"There are too many tools, it's too technical."

The overwhelm comes from copying someone else's stack piece by piece. You don't need to keep up with the tools; you need one home base and one workflow. Everything in this workbook happens inside the app you already pay for.

05

After the parallel week

Schedule the working version.

Both platforms interview you. Start with read-only access. Grant sending or writing only after a week of watching the workflow.

Claude

Schedule > New Task starts a setup conversation, then asks for the task type, cadence, time, and notifications. Two flavors exist: cloud routines (minimum one hour apart, each run starts fresh) and local scheduled tasks in the desktop app, which can use everything on your machine as long as it is awake.

ChatGPT

The Schedule surface offers an interview-style setup prompt. Tasks run on a clock or as monitors that check periodically and notify you only on a meaningful change, at most once per hour.

ChatGPT schedule setup"Let's set up a scheduled task. First explain how scheduled tasks work in ChatGPT, then interview me to figure out what I need to schedule and when it should run."
06

Operating plan

Four weeks

Week 1

Run alongside yourself.

Compare the manual and machine versions each day.

Week 2

Add the check and stop condition.

Turn every gap into an instruction. Stop and ask when unsure.

Week 3

Schedule and sample.

Put the stable briefing on a clock and review a sample.

Week 4

Build the second workflow.

Return to your ranked list and take the next task through the same process.

From the workshop"I don't have time to set this up."

I know it can feel like one more project on the pile. But this is the only place in your business where the exchange rate is that good: an evening of setup buys back hours every single week, forever. That's also why the rule is one workflow, not five. Don't build five this week. Build one and run it honestly for seven days.

07

Client data

Disclose AI processing.

Put a clause in your agreements saying client information is stored in and processed by the AI tools you use to deliver the work. Seek legal advice for the wording in your country.

From the workshop"I can't trust it with client work."

Correct as an observation, wrong as a conclusion. Trust is the output of the process, not the entry fee: the machine drafts, it checks its own work against a checklist you wrote once, and nothing leaves without your signature. Tell your clients how their information is processed, and grant every connection the smallest access it needs. Small access, small mistakes.

Power user mode

Build for durability.

Brand-kit folder anatomy

brand-kit/
  AGENTS.md          the law: always use tokens, never invent values
  tokens.css         colors, fonts, spacing, corner radius, shadows
  templates/         deck.html, proposal.html, landing-page.html
  assets/            logos, fonts
  examples/          two past outputs that were approved

In Claude Code or Codex, keep the short law file at `AGENTS.md`. It points every build at the shared tokens and templates, so the rules load with the work.

Proposal from a meeting transcript

  1. Give the workflow the meeting transcript.
  2. Add past proposals, pricing rules, and the brand kit.
  3. Create a designed proposal from the matching template.
  4. Run a self-review checklist pass. Trace every number to the transcript or pricing rules. Mark unsupported points for your check.
  5. Review, change what only you can know, and approve.
  6. Create a draft email with the proposal attached. Leave it unsent.
